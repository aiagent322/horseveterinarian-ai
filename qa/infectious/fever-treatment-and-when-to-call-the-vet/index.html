Infectious Disease & Fever · Q&A

How is fever treated, when should I call the vet, and what are the complications?

🚨

If your horse has a fever, a weak or wobbly hind end, trouble breathing or swallowing, or swollen jaw lymph nodes, call your veterinarian right away and isolate the horse. This page is educational and cannot assess your horse.

Urgent: this can become life-threatening quickly. If your horse is unwell now, call your veterinarian first, then use this page.

Short answer

EQUUS says to call your veterinarian for a high fever, a fever that does not respond to cold hosing, or one that lasts several days or comes with other signs of illness. Fans and cold hosing can help cool the horse. Never exceed prescribed medication doses.

When to call the vet (EQUUS; Kentucky)

  • A high fever, defined by EQUUS as 3 or more degrees above normal, or one that does not respond to cold hosing.
  • A fever that persists several days or comes with other signs of illness.
  • An elevated or unusually low temperature with other signs of illness (Kentucky).
  • A fever of 105°F or higher prompts consideration of serious causes such as strangles, anaplasmosis, Potomac horse fever and some viruses (EQUUS).

Treatment

A mild fever 2 to 3 degrees above normal, lasting a day or two, usually does no harm; rest and recheck periodically (EQUUS). Vets often treat fever mainly when the horse is miserable and not eating or drinking. Fans, cold hosing and cool IV fluids can help cool the horse. EQUUS warns that medication overdoses are toxic, that it is riskier when a horse is not eating or drinking, and that doses must come from your vet. Cause-specific treatment depends on the diagnosis.

Complications

Very high or prolonged fever can increase calorie and water use, leading to weight loss and dehydration; the main practical risk is that a miserable horse stops eating and drinking (EQUUS). Fever above 106°F or any prolonged fever can cause physiological harm. See our fever emergency guide.

Sources

  • EQUUS — What to do when your horse has a fever
  • University of Kentucky Extension — Evaluating the Health of Your Horse
